Start Here

Before changing a relationship structure, slow down enough to understand what you are actually trying to create.

Name your why

What do you hope will become more possible: autonomy, exploration, intimacy, community, honesty, sexual variety, emotional connection, or something else? Different people in the same relationship can have different reasons.

Separate structure from quality

A label such as monogamous, open, polyamorous, or relationship-anarchist does not create security by itself. Security is built through behavior: responsiveness, reliability, consent, honesty, repair, and respect.

Expect a transition, not a switch

Changing agreements can expose old conflicts, attachment fears, assumptions, or power differences. Treat the process as a gradual redesign rather than a one-time decision.

Is opening the relationship a good step right now?

Non-monogamy is not inherently a problem, but timing, consent, relational stability, skills and available bandwidth matter. Use this as a pause-and-reflect screen, not a pass/fail test.

Relationship Values

Move from inherited rules to consciously chosen values.

Question binary thinking

Relationships rarely fit neatly into right/wrong or success/failure categories. Distinguish ethical concerns such as deception, coercion, or abuse from ordinary differences in preference.

Choose your core values

Common values include consent, transparency, personal responsibility, autonomy, compassion, reliability, sexual health, fairness, privacy, and freedom.

Define your words

Partners can use the same words—sex, dating, love, commitment, fidelity, privacy—while meaning different things. Explicit definitions prevent avoidable conflict.

Relationship Structures

There is no single correct relationship structure. The task is to make power, expectations, and tradeoffs visible.

Monogamy and variations

Monogamy can be deliberately designed rather than assumed. Some couples define flirtation, pornography, close friendships, fantasy, or occasional exceptions differently.

Consensual non-monogamy

This umbrella includes many structures in which everyone affected is aware of and consents to multiple romantic and/or sexual connections.

Hierarchy and power

Descriptive priorities may emerge from children, finances, caregiving, housing, or history. Prescriptive hierarchy can give some partners control over relationships they are not part of. Ask who has voice, who bears risk, and who can make decisions.

Metamours and networks

You do not have to be close friends with a partner’s other partners. A workable goal is respectful, boundaried coexistence with clear channels for practical information and conflict.

Consent & Boundaries

Consent is active, informed, specific, reversible, and free of pressure. Boundaries describe what you will or will not participate in.

Consent is collaborative

Good consent includes the well-being of everyone meaningfully affected. “Yes” obtained through fear, guilt, threats, intoxication, or persistent pressure is not meaningful consent.

Boundary vs. agreement

A boundary is about your participation: “I will not have barrier-free sex under these conditions.” An agreement is a shared commitment: “We will tell each other before changing our sexual-health plan.” Rules that try to control a third person deserve extra scrutiny.

Yes / Maybe / No

A useful exercise is to sort activities or relationship experiences into Yes, Maybe, and No. For each Maybe, identify the conditions that would make exploration feel safe enough and the plan for stopping.

Revisit over time

Preferences and limits change. Consent and agreements require ongoing check-ins, not a one-time signature.

Agreement Builder

Make agreements concrete enough that everyone can tell whether they are being kept.

Write behavior, not feelings

“Do not fall in love” is difficult to define or enforce. Behavioral agreements—communication, scheduling, sexual-health practices, privacy, overnight plans—are clearer.

Aim for mutual benefit

An agreement should address the underlying needs of all affected people rather than simply reduce one person’s anxiety at everyone else’s expense.

Build in revision

Set a review date. Ask what is working, what is too restrictive, what is too vague, and what new information has emerged.

Be cautious with vetoes

A veto can create short-term reassurance but can also place major power over one relationship in the hands of someone outside it. Explore the fear underneath the veto request and look for less coercive protections.

Attachment & Security

Non-monogamy can amplify attachment dynamics because there are more transitions, separations, reunions, and uncertainties.

Notice your strategy under stress

Some people pursue reassurance and over-focus on partners; others distance, minimize needs, or shut down. Some alternate between both. These are patterns, not identities.

Balance autonomy and connection

Healthy security allows closeness without self-abandonment and independence without emotional disappearance.

Safe haven

Security includes being able to turn toward a partner for comfort, acceptance, and support when distressed.

Secure base

Security also includes encouragement to explore, grow, take appropriate risks, and maintain a life beyond the relationship.

Six anchors

Practice presence, expressed appreciation, emotional attunement, reliable rituals, repair after conflict, and secure attachment with yourself.

Jealousy Explorer

Jealousy is often a cluster of emotions rather than a single feeling. Treat it as information, not proof that someone has done something wrong.

Deconstruct the feeling

Ask what is underneath: fear of abandonment, comparison, loss of time, sexual insecurity, exclusion, status threat, uncertainty, grief, envy, or an unmet need.

Regulate before negotiating

When highly activated, first reduce physiological arousal. A conversation held after regulation is usually more productive than one held in panic.

Ask for reassurance specifically

Instead of “make me feel better,” ask for something observable: a check-in text, a plan for reconnecting, affection before departure, clarity about timing, or protected quality time.

Do not trade away consent

Do not agree to sex, contact, or relationship arrangements you do not want just to reduce someone else’s jealousy.

Communication & Check-ins

The goal is not perfect communication. The goal is enough honesty and repair capacity to keep learning together.

Use self-responsible language

Describe your own experience, feelings, needs, and requests instead of presenting interpretations of another person as facts.

One issue at a time

When a conflict expands into every past injury, pause and return to one current problem that can actually be addressed.

Schedule hard conversations

Choose a time when people are not rushing, intoxicated, exhausted, or immediately leaving for another date. Time-limited conversations can prevent spirals.

Regular relationship review

A brief recurring check-in can cover appreciations, current stress, time/attention, agreements, sexual health, upcoming changes, and one request for the next week.

Conflict & Repair

Rupture is inevitable. Security grows from how people return to connection.

Pause without abandoning

If escalation is high, state that you need a break and agree on when you will return. Silent disappearance can intensify attachment distress.

Shift from blame to problem-solving

The past cannot be changed. Identify what happened, acknowledge impact and responsibility, then design a different response for next time.

Repair can be simple

A useful repair may include: “I see the impact,” “I understand what you needed,” “Here is what I own,” “Here is what I will do differently,” and “What would help now?”

Seek help for stuck patterns

Recurrent high-intensity conflict, coercion, abuse, chronic deception, or trauma reactions may require professional support. Relationship structure changes should not be used to bypass an unsafe or severely distressed core relationship.

Rituals, Time & Transitions

Predictability and intentional connection matter, especially when time is divided across multiple relationships.

Create reliable touchpoints

Standing dates, weekly calls, morning rituals, or predictable check-ins can reduce ambiguity and help partners feel included in each other’s lives.

Attend to departures and reunions

Leaving for another date and returning afterward can activate attachment systems. Decide what kind of goodbye, information, privacy, and reconnection each person prefers.

Protect existing bonds without freezing growth

Nurture established relationships while allowing new relationships to develop. Do not assume that new relationship energy means an older bond is less valuable.

Make room for separate lives

Secure relationships support individual friendships, interests, work, rest, and solo time.

Sexual Health & Information Sharing

Sexual-health agreements should be explicit, realistic, and updated when circumstances change.

Clarify what must be disclosed

Decide what information partners need about new contacts, barrier use, testing, symptoms, exposure concerns, and changes in risk.

Avoid shame-based framing

Risk can be reduced but not eliminated. Treat sexual health as a shared planning task rather than a morality test.

Use qualified medical care

For testing frequency, contraception, PrEP/PEP, vaccines, symptoms, pregnancy concerns, or exposure questions, use individualized guidance from a qualified clinician.

Secure Relationship With Self

A partner can support regulation, but no partner can be the sole source of identity, worth, calm, or meaning.

Self-attunement

Pause and ask: What am I feeling? What does my body notice? What do I need? What story am I telling myself? What is actually known?

Self-soothing and support

Build multiple ways to regulate: rest, movement, grounding, journaling, friends, therapy, creative work, time in nature, and appropriate partner support.

Maintain boundaries

Security with self includes the ability to say no, tolerate another person’s disappointment, and stay connected to your values.

Grow your own life

A secure base also exists internally: encourage your own goals, friendships, competence, interests, and future plans.

Transitions & Endings

Relationships can change form without being failures.

Expect change

A romantic or sexual relationship may become friendship, co-parenting, community, or a more distant connection. Not every transition must end in total cutoff.

Grieve what is ending

Even consensual changes can involve loss. Make room for grief rather than forcing immediate positivity.

Do not recruit sides

When safe and appropriate, avoid turning shared friends, children, or metamours into judges. Abuse, coercion, or safety threats are different and require protection first.

Redesign deliberately

If a relationship changes, clarify contact, shared responsibilities, privacy, community events, sexual boundaries, and what each person needs to move forward.
